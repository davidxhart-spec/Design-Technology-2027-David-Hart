A3.4 — Introduction to electronic systems (HL)

Key concepts

Electronic systems move, process, and respond to information using electric current. Almost every modern product contains electronics — phones, appliances, cars, toys, medical devices. Understanding basic electronic architecture is essential for any designer working beyond purely mechanical products.

The input–process–output model

Every electronic system can be described as three subsystems:

  • Input — sensors that detect physical quantities (light, temperature, pressure, motion) and convert them to electrical signals. Examples: LDR, thermistor, switch, microphone.
  • Process — circuits that manipulate the signal (amplify, filter, compare, calculate). Examples: op-amp, logic gates, microcontroller.
  • Output — actuators or indicators that convert electrical signals back to physical effects. Examples: LED, motor, buzzer, speaker.

Analysing a product as input–process–output makes complex systems understandable and modular.

Analogue and digital systems

Every electronic system is either analogue, digital, or a mixture of the two.

An analogue system uses a signal that varies continuously — it can take any value within its range. A classic analogue signal is a sine wave: a smooth oscillation described by its amplitude and its frequency. A moving-coil speaker, a potentiometer volume control and a thermocouple are all analogue: the quantity varies smoothly and the circuit responds smoothly.

A digital system uses discrete values — in practice two, represented as 0 and 1 (binary). A digital signal is a square wave that is either high or low, with nothing meaningful in between. Logic gates, microcontrollers and memory are digital.

The practical difference: analogue signals carry infinite detail but degrade with noise and distance, while digital signals carry only the detail you sampled but can be copied and transmitted without loss. Most real products are mixed — a sensor produces an analogue voltage, an analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) turns it into numbers, a microcontroller processes it, and a digital-to-analogue converter drives an analogue output.

Feedback: open- and closed-loop systems

The input–process–output model above is only half the story. Many systems add a feedback path that measures the output and reports it back to the process.

An open-loop system acts without checking the result. A toaster runs its heating element for the time you dial in; it does not measure how brown the toast is. A basic desk fan runs at the speed you select regardless of room temperature. Open-loop systems are simple, cheap and predictable — and wrong whenever conditions change.

A closed-loop system measures the output and adjusts. A room thermostat compares measured temperature to the target and switches the heating accordingly. Cruise control measures road speed and varies throttle to hold it on a hill. A 3D printer measures hot-end temperature and modulates the heater to hold 210 °C.

The purpose of feedback in a closed loop is to hold the output at the desired value despite disturbances — a draught, a hill, a change in load. That is the entire reason to accept the extra sensor, extra cost and extra complexity. Ask of any product: does it measure what it is doing? If not, it is open-loop, and it will drift whenever the world changes around it.

Designing electronics responsibly

Electronics are now in almost every product, which makes the designer's choices consequential at scale. Three demands sit on any electronic product: it must be safe, energy-efficient, and built to use minimal energy in operation.

  • Safe — isolation of mains voltages, protection against overheating and short circuit, secure battery containment (lithium cells fail dangerously), and materials that do not release harmful substances in use or in a fire.
  • Energy-efficient — the efficiency of the conversion itself. A switch-mode power supply wastes far less as heat than a linear one; an LED delivers far more light per watt than a filament.
  • Minimal energy — how much the product draws in normal life, including when idle. Standby draw is the classic failure: a device that spends 23 hours a day waiting, consuming power the user never notices.

Beyond use, electronics create the fastest-growing waste stream in the world. Design decisions determine whether a product becomes e-waste early: whether the battery can be replaced, whether the case can be opened without destroying it, whether components are separable for recovery, and whether firmware support outlasts the hardware. A device that still works but can no longer be updated or repaired is scrapped just as surely as a broken one.

Basic components

  • Resistor (R) — limits current. Measured in ohms (Ω). Colour bands indicate value.
  • Capacitor (C) — stores charge. Smooths signals, times circuits. Measured in farads (F, usually µF or pF).
  • Diode — allows current in one direction only. Used in rectifiers, protection circuits, LEDs.
  • Transistor — amplifies or switches current. The foundation of all modern electronics.
  • LED — light-emitting diode; visual output with low power.
  • IC (integrated circuit) — many components on a single chip (op-amp, timer, microcontroller).

Ohm's Law and power

V = I × R (voltage = current × resistance). Fundamental relationship.

P = V × I (power = voltage × current, measured in watts).

These two equations let you calculate any unknown given two knowns. Essential for choosing resistors, predicting battery life, and sizing components.

Logic gates and digital systems

Digital systems use only two voltage levels: HIGH (1) and LOW (0). Logic gates combine these:

  • AND — output HIGH only if all inputs HIGH
  • OR — output HIGH if any input HIGH
  • NOT (inverter) — output opposite of input
  • NAND, NOR, XOR — combinations of the above

Complex digital behaviour is built from networks of gates. A microprocessor contains billions of them.

Microcontrollers

A microcontroller is a programmable IC containing a processor, memory, and input/output pins. Designers write code (in C, MicroPython, or visual languages) that reads sensors, makes decisions, and drives outputs. Arduino and Raspberry Pi Pico are popular prototyping platforms. A single microcontroller can replace hundreds of discrete components.

Sensors and actuators

Sensors convert physical quantities into electrical signals: - LDR (light), thermistor (temperature), ultrasonic (distance), accelerometer (motion), hall-effect (magnetic field), force-sensing resistor (pressure).

Actuators convert electrical signals into physical action: - DC motor (rotation), servo (precise angle), solenoid (linear pull), piezo buzzer (sound), LED (light), stepper motor (incremental rotation).

Circuit design and simulation

Modern designers draft circuits in software before soldering:

  • Schematic capture — draw the circuit diagram (KiCad, EasyEDA, Fritzing).
  • Simulation — run the circuit virtually to check behaviour (LTspice, Tinkercad Circuits).
  • PCB layout — convert schematic to a printable circuit board.

Simulation catches errors before hardware is built, saving time and components.

Case studies

Smart thermostat (Nest, Ecobee) — sensors (temperature, occupancy, humidity) feed a microcontroller that processes data against learned schedules and user preferences, outputting signals to HVAC relays. Input-process-output at product scale.

Arduino-based prototypes — have democratised electronic product prototyping. A $25 board, a handful of sensors, and a breadboard let designers test electronic ideas in an evening.

Electric toothbrush — timer IC drives a small DC motor through a H-bridge transistor circuit, with a pressure sensor input that reduces intensity when the user presses too hard. LED indicators signal battery and operating mode.

Automatic hand sanitiser dispenser — infrared distance sensor (input), comparator or microcontroller (process), small pump and LED (output). A textbook IPO example.

Glossary

  • Analogue — a signal that varies continuously and can take any value in its range.
  • Digital — a signal restricted to discrete values, in practice binary 0 and 1.
  • Sine wave — the characteristic smooth oscillation of an analogue signal.
  • Open-loop system — a system that acts without measuring its output.
  • Closed-loop system — a system that measures its output and adjusts to hold a target.
  • Feedback — the path returning output information to the process, enabling closed-loop control.
  • Input — component or subsystem that converts physical quantity to electrical signal.
  • Process — circuit or code that manipulates signals.
  • Output — component that converts electrical signal to physical action.
  • Resistor — passive component limiting current.
  • Capacitor — component storing electrical charge.
  • Transistor — semiconductor that amplifies or switches current.
  • Diode — component allowing current flow in one direction.
  • LED — light-emitting diode.
  • IC — integrated circuit; many components on one chip.
  • Microcontroller — programmable IC with processor, memory, and I/O pins.
  • Sensor — component converting a physical quantity into an electrical signal.
  • Actuator — component converting an electrical signal into physical action.
  • Logic gate — digital circuit performing a Boolean operation (AND, OR, NOT).
  • Ohm's Law — V = I × R.

Check your understanding

1. Describe the input–process–output model and apply it to an automatic bathroom light.

Every electronic system can be divided into an input (detecting a physical quantity), process (making decisions or transformations), and output (producing a physical effect). For an automatic bathroom light: input is a passive infrared (PIR) motion sensor; process is a timer circuit or microcontroller that switches on for a set period after movement is detected; output is an LED or filament lamp providing illumination. This modular view makes complex products designable in stages.

2. Using Ohm's Law, calculate the current through a 220 Ω resistor connected to a 5 V supply.

I = V / R = 5 / 220 = 0.0227 A = 22.7 mA. This is typical of an LED-limiting resistor, keeping the current safely below the LED's maximum rating (~20 mA for standard LEDs).

3. Explain the function of a microcontroller and why it often replaces discrete component circuits in modern products.

A microcontroller is a small programmable IC containing a processor, memory, and I/O pins. It reads signals from connected sensors, makes decisions in software, and drives outputs — replacing what would otherwise be a large assembly of logic gates, timers, and comparators. The advantage is flexibility: a single microcontroller can be re-programmed for different products or updated later, whereas discrete circuits are fixed at manufacture. Cost, size, and power consumption have all dropped sufficiently that microcontrollers now appear in children's toys.

4. Identify and describe three sensors and three actuators you might use in a student-designed "smart plant pot" that waters itself.

Sensors: soil moisture sensor (detects dryness), light sensor/LDR (detects sunlight levels), temperature sensor (detects ambient conditions). Actuators: small peristaltic pump (delivers water), LED indicator (status light), piezo buzzer (alert when water reservoir is empty). A microcontroller reads the sensors, compares to thresholds, and drives the actuators accordingly.

Task 1 — Electronics in everyday products and responsible use

Learning outcomes

  • Describe the input–process–output model of an electronic system.
  • Identify electronic systems embedded in everyday products.
  • Explain e-waste, planned obsolescence, RoHS/WEEE and CE safety marking.
  • Evaluate a real product against responsible-design criteria.

1. What is an electronic system?

Every electronic product, however simple, follows the same pattern: an input senses something in the world, a process stage (a circuit or microcontroller) decides what to do with that information, and an output acts on the decision. A kettle senses temperature and switches off a heating element; a phone senses a touch and lights a pixel; a washing machine senses water level and drives a motor. Keep this input–process–output model in mind — Tasks 2–6 build up each stage in detail.

List five products you own or use daily. For each, write one sentence identifying its input, its process and its output.

2. Responsible use of electronics

Designers of electronic products carry responsibilities beyond making the product work. Research each term below and write a two- to three-sentence definition in your own words:

  • E-waste — discarded electrical and electronic equipment; one of the fastest-growing waste streams globally, much of it containing recoverable metals and hazardous substances.
  • Planned obsolescence — designing a product to have an artificially short useful life (non-replaceable batteries, unrepairable seals, software that slows or stops supporting older hardware).
  • Right to repair — the movement and, in some regions, legislation requiring manufacturers to make parts, tools and documentation available so products can be repaired rather than replaced.
  • RoHS (Restriction of Hazardous Substances) — an EU directive restricting substances such as lead, mercury and cadmium in electronic products.
  • WEEE (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) — an EU directive governing the collection, recycling and recovery of electronic waste.
  • CE marking and low-voltage safety standards — marks and standards confirming a product meets minimum safety requirements for sale (insulation, fusing, protection against electric shock).

  • Estimate its expected lifespan. What would most likely cause it to be thrown away — failure, damage, or the user simply wanting a newer model?
  • Overall, would you call this a responsibly designed product? Give two reasons.

Task 2 — Analogue and digital signals, input devices and sensors

Learning outcomes

  • Distinguish analogue from digital signals.
  • Explain why microcontrollers need analogue-to-digital conversion.
  • Identify common input devices and sensors and what physical quantity each detects.

1. Analogue and digital signals

An analogue signal varies continuously over a range (for example a temperature sensor's output voltage rising smoothly as it warms). A digital signal has only discrete states, almost always just two: HIGH (logic 1, close to the supply voltage) and LOW (logic 0, close to 0 V).

Most real-world quantities — light, sound, temperature, pressure — are analogue. A microcontroller's processor, however, only understands digital values. An analogue-to-digital converter (ADC), usually built into the microcontroller, samples the analogue voltage at regular intervals and converts each sample to a binary number the processor can use. Sketch a simple analogue voltage–time graph, then sketch the stepped digital approximation an ADC would produce alongside it.

Task 3 — Electrical calculations and measuring tools

Learning outcomes

  • Apply V = IR and P = IV to solve circuit problems.
  • Explain what a multimeter and an oscilloscope each measure and how.
  • Read a simple oscilloscope trace.
  • Solve multi-step calculations involving current, voltage, resistance and power.

1. The three key equations

Learn these, and be able to rearrange them:

  • V = IR
  • P = IV
  • P = I²R

where V = voltage (volts, V), I = current (amps, A), R = resistance (ohms, Ω) and P = power (watts, W).

2. Worked examples

  • Example 1: a 12 V supply is connected to a 4 Ω resistor. Find the current. I = V / R = 12 / 4 = 3 A
  • Example 2: a component draws 0.5 A from a 9 V supply. Find its power consumption. P = IV = 0.5 × 9 = 4.5 W
  • Example 3: a 6 W bulb operates at 12 V. Find the current it draws and its resistance. I = P / V = 6 / 12 = 0.5 A; R = V / I = 12 / 0.5 = 24 Ω

3. Measuring tools

Research and summarise, in your own words:

  • Multimeter — a handheld instrument that measures voltage (voltmeter mode, connected in parallel across a component), current (ammeter mode, connected in series in the circuit) and resistance (ohmmeter mode, measured on a component with the power removed). Note the safety rule: never measure resistance on a live circuit.
  • Oscilloscope — displays a graph of voltage (y-axis) against time (x-axis), so you can see a signal's shape, amplitude, frequency and whether it is analogue (smooth or sloped) or digital (square steps). Look up an image of a sine-wave trace and a square-wave trace and sketch both, labelling the y-axis (V) and the x-axis (time).

4. Practice questions

  1. A resistor of 220 Ω has 5 V across it. Calculate the current flowing through it.
  2. A motor draws 2 A at 6 V. Calculate its power.
  3. An LED requires a current of 20 mA when 2 V is dropped across it. Calculate the resistance of the resistor needed in series to drop the remaining voltage from a 9 V supply. (Hint: find the voltage the resistor must drop first, then apply V = IR.)
  4. A component is rated at 3 W and operates at 12 V. Calculate the current it draws.
  5. Explain why an ammeter must be connected in series but a voltmeter in parallel.
  6. Sketch the multimeter dial setting and probe placement you would use to check whether a 9 V battery is still delivering close to 9 V.

5. Exit task

A torch uses two 1.5 V batteries in series to power an LED bulb rated at 3 V, 0.06 A. Calculate (a) the total supply voltage, (b) the power delivered to the bulb, and (c) say which multimeter setting and connection method you would use to confirm the current drawn in the real circuit. Show all working.

Task 4 — Resistors, capacitors, and circuit and flow diagrams

Learning outcomes

  • Calculate combined resistance for resistors in series and parallel.
  • Calculate combined capacitance for capacitors in series and parallel.
  • Draw and interpret block (system) diagrams.
  • Draw a flow diagram for simple control logic.

1. Resistors in series and parallel

Series: the total resistance is the sum of the resistances.

Rtotal = R1 + R2 + R3

Parallel: the reciprocal of the total equals the sum of the reciprocals.

1 / Rtotal = 1 / R1 + 1 / R2 + 1 / R3

Work through: two 100 Ω resistors in series (answer: 200 Ω); two 100 Ω resistors in parallel (answer: 50 Ω); a 60 Ω and a 30 Ω resistor in parallel (answer: 20 Ω).

2. Capacitors in series and parallel

Capacitors behave the opposite way to resistors.

Parallel: the total capacitance is the sum.

Ctotal = C1 + C2 + C3

Series: the reciprocals add.

1 / Ctotal = 1 / C1 + 1 / C2 + 1 / C3

Work through: two 100 µF capacitors in parallel (answer: 200 µF); two 100 µF capacitors in series (answer: 50 µF). Write one sentence explaining why the series and parallel rules swap between resistors and capacitors.

3. Circuit arrangement and system diagrams

A system (block) diagram shows the flow of information or energy through a product without drawing every component — typically boxes for input, process and output, joined by arrows. A circuit diagram shows the actual components (resistors, capacitors, switches, ICs) and how they are wired.

Draw a system diagram for an automatic porch light: box 1 "LDR (light sensor)" → box 2 "Microcontroller (compares light level to threshold)" → box 3 "Relay and lamp (output)". Label each arrow with what passes along it (a signal, not a wire).

4. Flow diagrams

A flow diagram (flowchart) represents the logic or sequence of decisions a system follows, using standard symbols: oval (start/end), rectangle (process/action), diamond (decision, always with a yes/no or true/false branch) and parallelogram (input/output).

Draw a flow diagram for a room light that should turn on automatically at night and only if a person is detected (using a light sensor and a PIR sensor). Include at least one decision diamond with two branches.

5. Exit task

A security light must switch on when it is dark and motion is detected, and switch off automatically after 30 seconds with no further motion. (a) Draw a system diagram showing inputs, process and output. (b) Draw a flow diagram showing the decision logic, including the 30-second timeout. (c) If the light uses two 5 W LED strips wired in parallel from a 12 V supply, calculate the total current drawn.

Task 5 — Microcontrollers, control and feedback systems

Learning outcomes

  • Explain the role of a microcontroller in an electronic system.
  • Distinguish open-loop from closed-loop (feedback) control.
  • Explain how a feedback system corrects itself.
  • Apply the input–process–output model to a control system with feedback.

1. What a microcontroller does

A microcontroller is a small computer on a single chip — a processor, memory and input/output pins — programmed to run one specific task repeatedly. It sits at the process stage of the input–process–output model: it reads input signals (from sensors, through its ADC where needed), runs stored instructions (a program) to decide what to do, and drives outputs (LEDs, motors, displays) accordingly. Examples you may recognise: Arduino boards, the Raspberry Pi Pico, the chip inside a washing machine's control panel.

List three reasons a microcontroller-based design might be chosen over a fixed (non-programmable) circuit for the same job. (Consider the cost of change, flexibility and the complexity of the logic required.)

2. Open-loop and closed-loop control

Open-loop control: the system produces an output from the input alone and does not check whether the output achieved the desired result. Example: a simple toaster timer that runs for a fixed time regardless of how brown the toast actually is.

Closed-loop control: the system measures its own output (or the effect of that output) and feeds that measurement back to adjust what it does next. Example: a thermostat-controlled heater that keeps checking the room temperature and switches the heater on and off to hold a target value.

For each of these, decide open-loop or closed-loop and justify: (a) a washing machine that runs a fixed 40-minute cycle regardless of load size; (b) a cruise-control system that holds a car at a set speed uphill and downhill; (c) a microwave that runs for the time you set on the dial.

3. Feedback systems

A closed-loop (feedback) system has four parts: a sensor (measures the actual output or condition), a comparator (compares the actual value with the desired, target value, giving the error), a controller (decides the correction needed) and an actuator (applies the correction). The loop repeats continuously.

Draw this as a system diagram, with an arrow looping back from the sensor to the comparator, for a room thermostat: the target temperature is set by the user → the comparator checks it against the sensor's current reading → the controller switches the heater relay → the heater changes the room temperature → the sensor detects the new temperature → the loop repeats.

4. Practice questions

  1. Define "microcontroller" in your own words.
  2. Explain one advantage of closed-loop control over open-loop control.
  3. A garden sprinkler runs for 10 minutes every morning at 7 a.m. whether or not it rained the night before. Is this open-loop or closed-loop? Justify your answer and suggest one sensor that could be added to improve it.
  4. Explain what "error" means in a feedback control loop.
  5. A cruise-control system is set to 100 km/h. The car starts climbing a hill and slows to 95 km/h. Describe, step by step, how the feedback loop responds.

5. Exit task

Design a closed-loop system for an automatic pet feeder that must dispense food only when the bowl is empty. Name the sensor, describe the comparator's decision and describe the actuator's action, in four or five sentences, using the four-part feedback structure from section 3.

Task 6 — Operational amplifiers and system design synthesis

Learning outcomes

  • Explain the function of an operational amplifier (op-amp) as a comparator and as an amplifier.
  • Recognise op-amp symbols and terminals.
  • Bring Tasks 1–5 together in a full electronic system design.
  • Justify design decisions against responsibility and sustainability criteria.

1. Operational amplifiers

An operational amplifier (op-amp) is an integrated circuit with two inputs — inverting (−) and non-inverting (+) — and one output. It has two common roles in design technology:

  • Comparator: compares the voltages on its two inputs. If the non-inverting input is higher, the output swings to its maximum ("high"); if the inverting input is higher, the output swings to its minimum ("low"). This is exactly the comparator in the feedback loop of Task 5 — comparing a sensor's voltage with a reference (threshold) voltage.
  • Amplifier: with resistors added in a feedback arrangement around the chip, it multiplies a small input voltage by a fixed gain to give a larger output voltage — useful for boosting a weak sensor signal (a microphone, or a small thermocouple) to a level a microcontroller's ADC can read accurately.

Sketch the standard op-amp symbol (a triangle) and label the inverting input, the non-inverting input, the output and the two power-supply pins.

2. Worked example

A light sensor (an LDR potential divider) outputs 0–3 V depending on brightness. A porch light should switch on once the voltage falls below 1.2 V (dusk). Explain, using the comparator function, how an op-amp with a fixed 1.2 V reference on one input and the sensor's voltage on the other would produce a digital HIGH/LOW output suitable for switching a relay — state which input the reference goes to, and why.
